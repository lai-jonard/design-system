la-disclosure

One expandable row: a question or heading that opens to its answer. A native <details> and <summary> underneath, so Tab reaches it, Enter and Space toggle it, a screen reader hears whether it is open, and find in page opens a closed one. Three variants: row for a list such as the FAQ, section for a heading inside a card, and quiet for a link-sized “More about this choice”. For depth one press away under content that stands on its own; never for what someone needs in order to act.

Disclosure or accordion

One row that opens is a disclosure. A set of rows stacked one under another is an accordion. la-disclosure is the one row; stack rows as siblings and they make an accordion, with no group behaviour of its own. That’s why a stack of rows looks like an accordion: it is one. Researched 9 October 2026; every source below opens.

Where we differ, and what it costs

  • No headings in a stack. APG and GOV.UK make each accordion header a heading, so people who move by headings can jump between them. Our FAQ rows aren’t headings and aren’t in a list, which APG’s FAQ uses instead. Decide this when the FAQ moves onto the system: a list round the rows, or heading semantics.
  • The chevron at the end. Carbon, Astryx and Fluent allow it; APG, GOV.UK and Apple put the marker first, and Carbon’s accessibility guidance says a chevron on the left helps people using magnification.
  • A heading inside the row: solved for section (9 Oct 2026). A heading inside <summary> isn’t reliably announced as a heading (Scott O’Hara; Adrian Roselli: JAWS doesn’t support it). Once the run’s sections became collapsible, section moved to W3C’s accordion structure: a real heading (heading-level) holding a button with aria-expanded, its body hidden="until-found" so find in page still opens it. row and quiet keep <details>.
  • A name clash. Shape of AI’s Disclosure pattern means labelling content made by AI. It has nothing to do with this component.

Anatomy

1 of 2 done

  1. Summary. The whole row is the control: a native <summary>, so the label, the trailing slot and the caret are pressed together and read together.
  2. Label. The summary slot, or the summary text when the slot is empty. Text, or an icon and a heading side by side; no interactive content.
  3. Trailing. The trailing slot, beside the caret: a status badge or a count. Row and section only.
  4. Caret. ph-caret-down in the muted colour; it turns over when the row opens. On a label that wraps it sits on the first line.
  5. Body. The default slot: what it reveals. It grows open, and once settled it stops clipping, so a menu inside can spill out.
  6. Hairline (row only). A line above each row, and below the last of its siblings.

Confirm you are adopting the written policy, and check your headcount

Once you employ five or more people, having a written health and safety policy is a legal must, and you have to bring it to everyone’s attention.

This is the headline decision: are you putting a written health and safety policy in place, and does the law require you to. The written-policy duty is staged by headcount. At five or more employees you must have a written statement of your general policy plus the organisation and arrangements for carrying it out, and you must bring it to everyone’s attention. Below five, the writing requirement is switched off, but nothing else is. A cluster of health and safety duties that bind from your very first hire, with no headcount threshold at all: the general duty to keep people safe so far as is reasonably practicable, the duty to make a suitable and sufficient risk assessment, the duty to appoint a competent person, and compulsory employers’ liability insurance.
  • Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 s.2(3)
  • Employers’ Health and Safety Policy Statements (Exception) Regulations 1975 (SI 1975/1584) reg 2 the exemption from the written-policy duty below five employees; the general duty and the duty to assess risk still apply below five

Set the risk profile the whole policy is written to

This policy is written for a low-risk, largely desk-based office or hybrid workplace, which is the typical startup.

The standard the law sets, so far as is reasonably practicable, scales the effort expected of you to the size of your risks, so a normal office is not held to a factory’s standard. If your business carries heavier risks, a workshop, a lab, a warehouse, field or site work, driving for work, lone working, hazardous substances, manual handling or machinery, the practical arrangements need materially more before you adopt it.

Name your competent person, and decide whether they sit inside or outside the company

Every employer, whatever its size, must appoint one or more competent people to help it meet its health and safety duties.

A person is competent if they have enough training, experience or knowledge to give you sound help. For a small, low-risk office this can be a founder or a manager who genuinely knows the ground, or an inexpensive external health and safety consultant. If you already have a genuinely competent person on the team, the law says appoint them ahead of an outsider.

Content guidance

What goes inside, how to write the label, and what can go wrong. Researched 9 October 2026.

What goes inside

  • Optional depth only. Never hide what most people need: GOV.UK says not to use Details to hide “information that the majority of your users will need”, and its accordion says the same. Nielsen’s rule for progressive disclosure: show the secondary only when asked; if people open it most of the time, the split is wrong.
  • One level. Never a disclosure inside a disclosure: more than two levels usually works badly (Nielsen), and GOV.UK says not to nest them.
  • Not something to read straight through. Content meant to be read in order shouldn’t be cut into panels (NN/g). A long FAQ answer belongs on its own page (Farrell, NN/g). No source gives a word limit; long panels on a phone mean a lot of scrolling and losing your place (NN/g).

The label

  • Say what’s inside, so people can tell whether they need to open it (GOV.UK Details; NN/g, accordions on desktop). Short, in sentence case, written like button text (GOV.UK accordion). WCAG 2.4.6 asks only that it be descriptive.
  • An FAQ asks the founder’s own question, the concern people actually contact you about (Farrell, NN/g).
  • “More about this choice” is generic. Naming the topic sets the expectation better (our reading of Nielsen; no source tests it). Worth raising with legal for the run’s questions.
  • A badge in the row is part of its name. Keep it to a word or two: GOV.UK notes extra summary text makes the button’s name longer for screen reader users.

The caret

  • In NN/g’s 2020 study, a caret was the only icon that did better than no icon at telling people the content opens in place; a plus or an arrow did no better than nothing. Icons were tested on the right only.
  • Friedman: people notice an icon on the right more than one on the left; use a down chevron or a plus, never an arrow pointing right. The icon and the label must do the same thing (NN/g).
  • Against: Carbon finds a left chevron helps people using magnification (above).

Notes

  • When to use. To keep depth one press away under content that stands on its own: an FAQ, legal’s explainer under a check, the rest of a framing paragraph, a long phase a founder may fold. Not for anything the founder must read to act (put it on the page), not to switch views (la-tabs), not for actions (la-menu).
  • Hairlines. The component draws them, because a page may place a host but not paint it. Don’t wrap each row in its own element: each would be the last of its type and draw both lines, doubling them. Don’t stack a row straight under something with a bottom border of its own. section and quiet draw no lines.
  • No group, and no “Show all”. None of the uses closes one row when another opens, so there is no la-disclosure-group; several can be open at once. The lists are short, so there is no “Show all sections” either.
  • A section is a heading holding a button (section, 9 Oct 2026). The component draws the heading at heading-level (default 3) round a button with aria-expanded and aria-controls, as W3C’s accordion pattern does, so people who move by headings can jump between a run’s sections. Put text (and an icon) in the summary slot, never a heading. Closed, the body is hidden="until-found" where the browser supports it, so find in page opens it; elsewhere it’s plainly hidden.
  • The label’s colour. A row’s label is in the secondary colour at rest and takes the text colour when open or under the pointer, so an open row reads as the one in hand. Our own; see Basis.
  • Motion. The caret turns on --dur-fast; the body grows from a 0fr grid track on --dur-base --ease-out, so nothing animates height. On close the track collapses first and the details shuts when it has. Under prefers-reduced-motion both change at once.
  • Targets. The row is 36px, the section 36px round a 28px icon, the quiet toggle 30px with its target stretched to 32px. On a touch screen the row and the section pad to 44px and the quiet target stretches to 44px.
  • Consumers. In this repo, all quiet: la-question’s What this means (decision 118, amended), la-step-questions’ What we read after a founder answers differently from what we filled in (decision 129), and la-playbook-step’s More. In new-design, the library FAQ in 05-playbooks.html, and the depth door and “More about this choice” that gen-runner.js and create-doc.js draw in the playbook run. The run’s phase head hasn’t moved onto it.
